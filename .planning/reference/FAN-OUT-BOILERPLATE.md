# Fan-out standard (maintainer's boilerplates, recorded 2026-11-21)

The maintainer's own prompts for upstream research. Use them, adjusted to the actual circumstances, whenever a milestone or phase prep says "fan out". They extend AGENTS.md "Milestone preparation" and "Phase workflow defaults". Provenance: pasted by the maintainer on 2026-11-21 as "elixir oss project fan out prompt.txt" and "general for each fan out prompt.txt"; lightly condensed, nothing dropped.

## Elixir OSS project fan-out

Discuss and consider all of it; for each decision fan out with subagents for context-window efficiency, and keep fanning out to the point of diminishing returns, enumerating every relevant point, to synthesize a coherent set of recommendations.

For each decision, research with subagents the pros, cons and tradeoffs of each approach with an example of each; what is idiomatic for Elixir, Plug, Ecto and Phoenix for this kind of library or app; lessons from other libraries and apps in the same space, including other languages and frameworks when they are popular and successful (what they did right, what they did wrong, the footguns); great developer ergonomics and user-friendliness. Immerse yourself, then one-shot a perfect set of recommendations so the maintainer does not have to think, all coherent with each other and moving toward the goals and vision of the project, using great software architecture and engineering, the principle of least surprise, and great UI/UX and developer experience where applicable.

Also consider everything in the repo's `prompts/` directory where applicable (best practices, pros and cons, tradeoffs, vision); where a newer brandbook exists, do not stress the old information. Take every relevant role's lens (software architecture, SWE, devops, SRE, and so on) with breadth and depth for every decision point.

If UI/UX or graphic design applies: consider the user persona, user flows and jobs to be done, and for each job the who, what, where, when and why (who can use it, what they can do, how, what they need, what they get back, which ties to the nouns, events and verbs of the domain language, where inputs come from and how they are used). Wise consistency in UI, UX, affordances, graphic design and motifs. Hide backend design and implementation details from the user unless a fundamental design or performance constraint requires exposing them; keep UI, UX and API design focused on the consumer, the user and the job, which makes the interface elegant and intuitive. Consider every design pillar (accessibility, performance and the others, enumerated against the project's goals), a design system that pays dividends, conventional components (least surprise), accessible, good in dark, light and system modes, no weirdness on hover or focus, following the brand book, great microcopy, plus creative direction and user psychology for the job in question. If it is not UI, discard that but keep the jobs. When designing APIs, take the consumer's perspective, not the provider's.

## General fan-out

For each decision point, consider breadth and depth through every relevant stakeholder-role lens (security, product, technical, design, and so on) with pros, cons, tradeoffs, examples, anti-patterns, patterns, best practices, footguns and lessons learned, including research online and insight from other products and ecosystems. Consider all angles and run an adversarial pass, then synthesize a perfect one-shot recommendation for each. Technical roles include software architect, devops, rendering engines, 2D and 3D graphics, distributed systems, and the language and framework specialists (Elixir, C, C++, TypeScript, Node, whatever applies). For UI/UX focus, stay on existing patterns, pros and cons, anti-patterns, design patterns, least surprise, conventions and elegant design, and do not get distracted. The point is to fan out, find a coherent set of recommendations, and follow them automatically.

House preference: **another copy and paste is better than another dependency.** If a dependency is truly needed, fine, but smaller, flatter dependency trees are more secure and avoid pulling in weird abstractions. Where applicable, add or run cheap consistency checks before the long test, process or suite; apply lessons learned, find the bottleneck and the low-hanging fruit, and take a step back to keep things moving instead of banging a head against the wall.

Adjust the details to the actual circumstances. Prefer reputable primary sources whenever possible, but also think for yourself.
